Sam researches social media and teen mental health

Starting the skill

Activation

SamI need to research social media and teen mental health for my sociology class.

The keyword "research" and a research project activate the skill. It opens with a short reminder that AI can make mistakes and that Sam should fact-check what it produces.

Phase 0 · Intake

Gathering context

The skill asks a few questions, one or two at a time. Sam explains:

  • An 8-page argumentative essay for a sociology class
  • Due in four weeks
  • At least five peer-reviewed sources, APA style
  • Sam has read a few news articles, but nothing academic
Exit checkThe skill has the context it needs. The research type is set to argumentative essay, so the working position will be a thesis. Because it's an 8-page paper requiring peer-reviewed sources, the skill sets a fairly demanding challenge level, without ever asking Sam's age or school.
Phase 1 of 5 · Explore and choose a direction

Briefing and mind map

The skill gives a short briefing: this is a heavily studied topic, most research asks whether social media harms teens, and the public debate is heated. It points out patterns, such as many studies relying on teens' estimates of their own screen time.

Then it opens the interactive mind map. Sam rotates it and clicks through the branches. The main "does social media cause depression?" branch is red, meaning crowded. "School phone bans" is less explored, and its narrower branches are cooler still.

Try it: drag to rotate, scroll to zoom, and click "School phone bans" to see what Sam saw.

SamI want to do school phone bans. My school just started one.
SkillWhat makes that more than a personal connection? Why might phone bans be worth researching right now?
SamBecause lots of schools are trying them, but there isn't much research yet on whether they work.

The skill flags that "Do phone bans work?" is too broad to answer in 8 pages, and asks what "work" would mean. Sam settles on social life rather than grades, because that branch was less explored.

Exit checkSam states the draft question in one sentence, in their own words: "How have school-day phone bans changed how high school students interact with each other in person?"
Phase 2 of 5 · Pre-research stress test

Facts corrected, reasoning questioned

The skill researches phone bans and social life more deeply and gives a second briefing. Sam starts forming a position.

SamPhone bans started after COVID, when everyone realized how addicted kids were.
Skill Factual correctionThat part isn't accurate. France passed a law restricting phones in primary and middle schools in 2018, before the pandemic. How might a longer history of bans be useful to your project?

That's a factual error, so the skill corrects it directly. When Sam makes a conceptual claim, the skill asks questions instead:

SamBans obviously improve social life. At my school, people talk at lunch now.
Skill Probing questionWhat else might have changed at your school at the same time? And how would you know whether what you're seeing at one school is true at others?

Later, Sam points out that some studies look at the same schools before and after a ban. The skill acknowledges that this is a strong point and explains why, then moves on to a weaker one: whether "talking more at lunch" is the same thing as better social life.

Exit checkSam writes a working thesis and names the strongest counterargument: bans may just move social conflict to after school instead of reducing it.
Phase 3 of 5 · Plan the research

Holding the line, kindly

SamCan you just write my sub-questions? I'm short on time.
SkillI can't write them for you, because they need to come from your thinking. But let's make it quick: what's one thing you'd need to know before you could answer your main question?

Sam writes three sub-questions, then decides for each where to look: education research databases, school district reports, and interviews or surveys of students.

Sam also lists what would support the thesis (before-and-after studies showing more in-person interaction) and what would complicate it (evidence that conflict moved online after school).

Exit checkSam has a research plan: sub-questions, where to look, and what would support or complicate the thesis.
Phase 4 of 5 · Research

The skill searches; Sam works with the results

The skill delivers:

  • the sources it found, with working links
  • a summary of what it found and didn't find (for example, almost no long-term studies yet)
  • suggestions for what Sam should look into further, including asking a librarian about two paywalled journals

Sam then sorts the sources by sub-question, evaluates the credibility of two of them before seeing the skill's assessment, and reflects: what's missing, what would make the research stronger, and what types of findings might weaken it.

To close, the skill encourages Sam to include a disclosure describing how AI was used in the research process, and mentions that Phase 5 is available whenever Sam wants it. Sam saves the research log.
Phase 5 · Ongoing stress testing (optional)

A week later

While writing, Sam uploads the research log and one new source: a news article reporting a school district survey.

SkillWelcome back. Last time you finished your research plan and sorted your sources. Before we start, can you restate your thesis in your own words?

Sam restates it. The skill asks for Sam's own read of the article's credibility first, then shares its own: the article reports a survey but doesn't say how many students responded or how they were chosen. It asks how much weight Sam's argument should place on it, and what Sam would need to find to rely on it.

Sam leaves with a stronger sense of which evidence can carry the argument, and with every word of the essay still Sam's own.
