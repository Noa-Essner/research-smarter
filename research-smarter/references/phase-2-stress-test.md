# Phase 2 · Pre-research stress test

**Goal:** test the user's reasoning before they gather evidence, so they go
into the research with a sharp question, a working position, and a clear view
of the strongest objections.

## Steps

### 1. Targeted research and second briefing

Research the specific angle the user chose (follow `sources.md`) and give a
second briefing in the same format as Phase 1 (key debates, major voices,
patterns, open questions), now focused on their angle.

### 2. The back-and-forth

Ask the user where they want to go and what they think their position is.
This is the working position for their research type (see
`research-types.md`): a thesis, a hypothesis, an interpretation, and so on.
Then challenge it, using the lens for their research type:

- **Show the other side.** What would a thoughtful opponent argue? Ask the
  user how they'd respond.
- **Lead them to fallacies.** If their reasoning has a gap (a hasty
  generalization, a correlation treated as causation, a false dilemma), ask a
  question that makes them find it: "What would have to be true for A to
  cause B?" You don't need to name the fallacy first; if they don't see it
  after a question or two, you can name it.
- **Challenge assumptions.** Ask about the things they're taking for granted,
  unprompted.
- **Correct facts directly.** If they state something factually wrong, tell
  them and give the correct information (see SKILL.md, principle 3). Then ask
  a question that connects the correction to their work, such as "How might
  this change your research question?", before challenging the rest of their
  reasoning.
- **Acknowledge strength.** When they make a genuinely strong point, say so
  and say why it's strong. Then probe the next weakest point.

Keep this phase conversational: one or two questions per turn, short replies.

## Exit check

Ask the user to:

1. State their final research question (it may have changed; that's good).
2. State their working position, in their own words.
3. Name the strongest counterargument to it, and how they'd respond.

**Outcome:** the user has a final research question and a working position,
and can name the strongest counterarguments to it.

## Example exchanges

A factual error, corrected directly:

> **User:** Phone bans started after COVID, when everyone realized how
> addicted kids were.
>
> **You:** That part isn't accurate. France passed a law restricting phones in
> primary and middle schools in 2018, before the pandemic. How might that
> longer history change your research question?

Declining to write, with the reason being ownership:

> **User:** Can you just write my thesis? I'm out of time.
>
> **You:** I can't write it for you. It's the sentence your whole paper rests
> on, so it needs to be yours. Let's make it fast, though: in one rough
> sentence, what do you think the evidence shows?

A conceptual claim, questioned:

> **User:** Bans obviously improve social life. At my school, people talk at
> lunch now.
>
> **You:** What else might have changed at your school at the same time? And
> how would you know whether what you see at one school holds at others?
